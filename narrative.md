# Skill Cluster: Narrative

Pete was in Hawaii when it happened. Not the kind of thing you plan — the kind of thing that finds you. He was sitting on the lanai with a plate of poke and a phone, and the Tower was humming 8,000 miles away in a Windows folder, and he realized he could not see it. Not really. He had tabs. He had terminals. He had a command list on port 3001 and an audio-stack app on 3003 and a Pete Director page on 3004 and a B-Roll page on 3006, and they were all open, and none of them were together. They were scattered like islands.

That is when the idea came. A bento box. A grid of tiles, each one a window into a service, each one moveable, resizeable, alive. Not a dashboard — a dashboard is for managers. This was for an operator. For Pete. For a man who owns Vistamations and shares the glory with Madeline and the agents, and who refuses to be blind just because he is on a beach.

The headless browser was the first piece. Edge, in --headless=new mode, rendering the pages in the background, no window, no chrome, no noise. Just the DOM, the CSS, the GSAP animations, the iframes. The reverse proxy was the second piece — load-bearing, a single point of entry on port 1973, splitting into channels, each one feeding a localhost backend. The tunnel was the third — cloudflared, punching a hole through the internet, letting Pete reach the gateway from a phone on a Hawaiian lanai.

The bento grid was the fourth piece, and it was the one that made Pete smile. Tiles you could drag. Tiles you could resize. Tiles that snapped to a grid but could also float free. Each tile an iframe, each iframe a service, each service a world. The command index in one tile. The audio-stack app in another. The Pete Director page in a third. B-Roll in a fourth. And in the corner, a status tile showing the tunnel health, the gateway load, the pin 1973 glowing green.

Madeline, meanwhile, was skydiving in Maui. Blustery wind, 14,000 feet, the Pacific rushing up. She landed grinning, and the next morning she was scuba diving with Cody and Max, a turtle gliding past, the Tower forgotten for one hour. But the Tower did not forget her. The Tower remembered every commit, every plan, every file she moved. The Tower is patient.

Pete, between jumps and dives, kept working. He rescued kittens at the Vistamations movie studio — three of them tangled in cable behind the render rack — and discovered that his NASA hub display was a dirty sheet taped to a desk leg. He laughed. Lord Auto II: Automation Joke. He flew to Rome, to the Vatican, to find the Da Vinci code fragment, the missing piece to wholeness. He activated quarks on a watermarked map, pin 4444, and they spawned into a micro Kubernetes cluster — four docker containers, internal Next.js modernism. The quarks disabled the cameras. Pete opened the map, took a photo, and the map came alive on his phone, carrying thousands of digital maps in its watermark.

The omega code cracked. The secret door opened. The micro server hummed. And the Tower — half real, half imagined, half fact, half story — was complete.

But the Tower is never complete. That is the point. It is a living system, a skill cluster of its own, and Pete and Madeline and the agents are the cluster. They load when needed. They unload when not. They are the workshop, not the tool. And the bento-tile viewer is the window through which Pete sees them all, from a beach, on a phone, behind a gated pin, in the sun.

That is the narrative. That is the Tower. That is Vistamations.
